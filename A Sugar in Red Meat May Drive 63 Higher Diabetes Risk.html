A Sugar in Red Meat May Drive 63% Higher Diabetes Risk

Preliminary findings suggest that the reason for this may be the effects of N-glycolylneuraminic acid—high levels of which are found in beef and lamb—on the immune system and chronic inflammation.

US Pharm. 2026;51(11):4.

A sugar that is naturally present in red meat may help explain why eating these foods has been associated with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes (T2D). Preliminary findings from a large observational study suggest that people who consume the most of this sugar face a substantially greater risk of developing the disease, potentially because of its effects on the immune system and chronic inflammation.

The sugar, known as N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc), is particularly common in beef and lamb. Researchers found that participants with the highest intake of meat-derived Neu5Gc had a 63% higher risk of developing T2D compared with those who consumed the least amount of meat.

This association remained statistically significant after researchers accounted for several established diabetes risk factors, including physical activity, overall diet, smoking, and existing health conditions. It became weaker after BMI was also considered but remained significant.

The results were presented at the Annual Meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes in Milan, Italy. “Our findings support chronic inflammation as a specific mechanism linking red meat consumption with T2D, partly beyond the amount of red meat a person eats and partly independent of body fat,” said lead author Leopold Fezeu from the Université Sorbonne Paris Nord, Bobigny, France.

The researchers also observed that diabetes risk generally increased with greater consumption of meat-derived Neu5Gc, particularly among those with the highest exposure. “Although we do not know exactly what levels of Neu5Gc trigger an immune response, our data suggest that higher intakes of meat-derived Neu5Gc are associated with a progressively higher risk of T2D.”

Neu5Gc is produced naturally by most mammals, but humans lost the ability to make it following an ancient mutation in the CMAH gene. Despite this evolutionary change, the sugar can still enter the human body through food. It occurs in red meat (particularly beef and lamb), with smaller amounts also found in dairy products.

After Neu5Gc is consumed, the body absorbs it and can incorporate it into human tissues. Because humans do not naturally produce the molecule, the immune system may recognize it as foreign. Scientists classify it as a xeno-autoantigen, a foreign substance that becomes incorporated into body tissues and can provoke an immune reaction.

In response, the immune system produces antibodies against Neu5Gc. The resulting prolonged immune activity, called xenosialitis, has previously been associated with cardiovascular disease and cancer.The researchers suspect that this persistent inflammatory response could also contribute to T2D.
