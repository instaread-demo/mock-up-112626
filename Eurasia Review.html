Reading The Sky In Stone: Teotihuacan And The Future Of Archaeology – Analysis

November 25, 2026November 25, 2026 Burak Oktenli 0 Comments

By Burak Oktenli

From ancient solar alignments to laser surveys and AI, humanity’s instruments have changed. The challenge of passing reliable observations to future generations has not.

On March 22, 1993, a photograph later published by archaeologist Ivan Šprajc captured the rising sun from the top of the Pyramid of the Sun at Teotihuacan, in central Mexico. Almost thirteen miles away, the flat summit of Cerro Colorado appeared nearly as wide as the solar disk. Viewed from above, the ancient city’s avenues can look uncannily like the circuits of a computer chip. But the sunrise photograph preserves something that the aerial resemblance cannot: the place from which the observation was made.

There is a temptation to describe the pyramids as ancient computers. Archaeological evidence has not established that they were. There is, however, a more rewarding scientific question inside that speculation: could the builders of a city make the passage of time visible, repeatable and teachable? Modern archaeology is giving us new ways to investigate that achievement, even as some of the landscape that made it possible is disappearing.

In 2003, heavy rain opened a hole near the Temple of the Feathered Serpent. Beneath it lay a human-made passage extending 103 meters. In one antechamber, archaeologists Sergio Gómez Chávez and Julie Gazzola documented more than 400 spheres bearing traces of altered pyrite. Traces of pyrite, hematite and magnetite adhered to the walls and ceiling. The excavators suggested that torchlight once made the minerals shimmer like stars. The builders had apparently recreated the night sky underground.

Near the end of the passage, four greenstone figures occupied the center of a rich offering. Several milliliters of liquid mercury were recovered from the deposit. Elsewhere, the floor had been shaped to evoke a miniature landscape. Mexico’s National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) interprets the tunnel as a constructed representation of the underworld, with mercury associated with its symbolic waters. The evidence brings us closer to the craftsmanship of its creators, although their experience of entering that space is harder to recover.

Mercury did eventually find a place in a computer. In 1949, Cambridge’s EDSAC used mercury-filled delay lines to store information. Electrical signals were converted into acoustic pulses, transmitted through the liquid, received and sent around again. The operation can be reconstructed from the components and their connections. The mercury at Teotihuacan was found in an offering. Its presence, without evidence of an equivalent mechanism, cannot establish a computing function.

Mica offers another instructive comparison. Today it is valued for its electrical insulating properties. At Xalla, a complex north of the Pyramid of the Sun, research by Linda Manzanilla and colleagues documents stacked mica sheets, possible tools for working them, and decorative plaques. The archaeological context helps explain the value of the material in that society. Its modern industrial applications are a different chapter in its history.

What could the pyramids themselves have enabled? Šprajc’s study of astronomical alignments identified two principal orientations in Teotihuacan’s street grid, approximately one degree apart. He proposed that the Pyramid of the Sun and the Ciudadela were oriented to mark recurring solar dates. In his reconstruction, sunsets around August 13 and April 30 could define an interval of about 260 days, corresponding to a major Mesoamerican calendrical cycle. The alignment can be measured and the astronomical calculation checked. The particular agricultural or ceremonial decisions made on those dates are less certain.

For comparison, in the Maya city of Xultun, in present-day Guatemala, researchers found astronomical tables painted on a room’s walls around the ninth century CE. Three legible lunar entries are separated by 177 or 178 days, approximately six lunar months. The numerical record provides direct evidence of reckoning. At Teotihuacan, by contrast, the buildings may have provided the physical setting in which observations were made and shared.

Today archaeologists can record such settings on a scale that would have been unimaginable a generation ago. Airborne lidar uses laser pulses to measure the terrain, producing a three-dimensional record of features that may be difficult to recognize on the ground. A 2021 study of the Teotihuacan Valley combined a 2015 lidar survey covering roughly 165 square kilometers with historical maps, surface surveys and excavation records. Its authors documented 205 archaeological features subsequently erased by mining operations. The digital survey preserved evidence of places that later researchers could no longer visit.

The same technology is helping recover the history of archaeology itself. In May 2026, researchers reported a drone-mounted lidar survey at Saqsaywaman, near Cusco in Peru. Subtle traces of old excavations, obscured by vegetation and erosion, could be located and placed within a modern map. A future researcher studying earlier work there will have a better chance of establishing where the measurements and artifacts came from.

Artificial intelligence may help examine these expanding archives. But on November 10, 2026, a researcher announced that AI agents had identified an apparently unmapped Maya site in Mexico. The next day he acknowledged that archaeologists had already documented it, as The Verge subsequently reported. The earlier regional lidar study had appeared in 2025. The episode illustrates the potential of automated search while showing that identifying a shape and establishing a new archaeological discovery are separate achievements. Local knowledge and earlier scholarship deserve a place in that process.

The future of archaeological science will also depend on what happens after the scan. On November 18, UNESCO announced a digitization partnership to preserve selected artworks and historical documents through high-resolution digital replicas. The initiative is not a new survey of Teotihuacan, but its commitment to delivering files in open formats offers a broader lesson for cultural heritage. A detailed record is most valuable when future generations can still open it, understand its origin and test the conclusions drawn from it. Sensitive archaeological locations, meanwhile, require appropriate protection rather than indiscriminate publication.

For a site such as Teotihuacan, that means preserving the survey date, coordinate system, original measurements and the evidence behind a reconstruction. A researcher should be able to distinguish an excavated feature from a digitally reconstructed one, and a measured solar alignment from a proposed ritual interpretation. Future software may offer explanations that today’s researchers have not considered. It needs access to the evidence that allows those explanations to be challenged.

Imagine teaching someone to recognize the return of a season. From the Pyramid of the Sun, you could point to a distant mountain whose summit seemed just wide enough to hold the rising sun. Your pupil could return a year later and repeat the observation without you. Nearly two millennia after the city’s builders set its monuments in place, that possibility remains one of its most remarkable gifts. Our most advanced instruments should be judged by whether they can leave future observers a gift of the same kind.
